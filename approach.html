Philosophy

My Approach

How I think about content, users, data, and what it means to document AI — in my own words.

Most documentation gets written to cover features. I'm interested in documentation that covers users. Those aren't the same thing.

A feature doc answers: what does this do? A user-centered doc answers: what are you trying to accomplish, where have you gotten stuck, and what do you need right now to keep going? The difference sounds philosophical until you look at the data. Then it becomes obvious which one is missing.

My starting point is always the same question: where are users actually failing?

Not where I think they're failing. Not where the product team thinks they're failing. Where the data says they're failing. Support tickets, completion rates, search queries that go nowhere — these are the places documentation hasn't reached someone who needed it. When I ran an ISQM analysis on SAP APM support cases, I found that 94.66% of tickets were closed without any KBA, FAQ, or help article contributing to the resolution. That's not a quality problem. That's a coverage problem. Nobody thought to write for those moments.

Data doesn't tell you what to write. It tells you what's missing. The writing still takes judgment — about what a person actually needs to know in the moment they're stuck, in language they'll trust, structured so they can act on it. Data narrows the target. The rest is craft.

Content has a cost when it fails. Not a soft cost. A real one.

A ticket opened. An engineer's time spent. A user's trust eroded. Every time someone searches for help and finds nothing, there's a cost that doesn't show up on any roadmap. I care about that cost. The goal isn't to write more documentation. It's to write the right documentation, for the right person, at the right moment.

With AI, the obligation changes. When you document a button, you're explaining a fixed behavior. When you document an agent, you're helping a user build a mental model of a system that makes judgment calls — judgment calls that may affect real decisions. That requires something different from feature coverage. It requires honesty about what the system cannot do, clarity about what "advisory" actually means, and specificity that helps someone decide whether to trust the output or verify it. That's not legal boilerplate. That's the actual job.

I've been working at the intersection of technical writing and data analysis for long enough to have a view on both. The writers who last are the ones who can read the data and still know that data doesn't write the doc. The analysts who matter are the ones who understand that a number without a human decision attached to it is just a number. I try to be both.

01

Write for the moment of need, not the moment of launch. Documentation lives or dies when a user is stuck at 11pm with a production issue, not when the product ships.

02

Data is the reality check, not the answer. It shows you where people are failing. It doesn't tell you how to help them. That still takes a human who understands the problem.

03

AI changes the writer's obligation. Scope, limitations, transparency, and human oversight aren't optional extras. They're the content that determines whether a user trusts the tool enough to use it.

04

A deflected ticket is a better outcome than a resolved one. The best documentation makes the support case unnecessary. That's the standard I write toward.
